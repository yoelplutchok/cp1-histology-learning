The circulatory system and the three layers of the heart wall

L1Learning
Which circulation works at high pressure and which at low pressure?
  1. A. Pulmonary circulation high; systemic circulation low
  2. B. Both run at the same high pressure, since both ventricles eject the same volume
  3. C. Systemic circulation high; pulmonary circulation low
AnswerC. Systemic circulation high; pulmonary circulation low
WhyThe left ventricle drives the high-pressure systemic circuit; the right ventricle drives the low-pressure pulmonary circuit through the lungs.
Explanation

Both ventricles eject the same volume each beat, but the resistance they pump against differs. The systemic circuit (left ventricle → aorta → body) has a high resistance, so it needs a high pressure (about 120/80 mmHg). The pulmonary circuit (right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → lungs) has a low resistance and runs at roughly a sixth of that pressure.

  • The reverse pairing swaps the two circuits.
  • Same pressure: equal volume does not mean equal pressure; pressure depends on the resistance downstream.

This is why the left ventricular wall is about three times thicker than the right.

L2Learning
Going outward from the heart, how are the arteries subdivided?
  1. A. Muscular artery → elastic artery → small artery → arteriole
  2. B. Elastic artery → muscular artery → small artery → arteriole
  3. C. Elastic artery → small artery → muscular artery → arteriole
AnswerB. Elastic artery → muscular artery → small artery → arteriole
WhyArteries shrink in steps: elastic (aorta) → muscular (distributing) → small artery → arteriole, which feeds the capillaries.
Explanation

The lecture's list of cardiovascular elements runs heart → arteries → capillaries → veins. The elastic arteries (aorta, pulmonary trunk) leave the heart; they branch into muscular arteries that distribute blood to organs, then into small arteries, and finally arterioles, the resistance vessels just before the capillaries. Veins run the other way: venules → small veins → medium-sized veins → large veins.

  • Putting muscular before elastic reverses the first step: the aorta, right at the heart, is the elastic one.
  • Putting small artery before muscular breaks the steady fall in size.

L3Learning
Name the three layers of the heart wall, from inside out.
  1. A. Epicardium → myocardium → endocardium
  2. B. Endocardium → myocardium → epicardium
  3. C. Endocardium → epicardium → myocardium
AnswerB. Endocardium → myocardium → epicardium
WhyInside out: endocardium (lining) → myocardium (muscle) → epicardium (outer covering, the visceral pericardium).
Explanation

Endo- = inside, myo- = muscle, epi- = upon. The endocardium lines the chambers and faces the blood; the myocardium is the thick muscular middle; the epicardium covers the outer surface and faces the pericardial cavity.

  • Epicardium → myocardium → endocardium is the right sequence read from outside in, not inside out.
  • Endocardium → epicardium → myocardium puts the covering layer in the middle; the muscle always sits between the two thin layers.

The heart wall is a modified vessel wall: endocardium ≈ tunica intima, myocardium ≈ tunica media, epicardium ≈ tunica adventitia.

Pericardium and epicardium

L4Learning
A shard of glass pierces the chest wall and enters the left ventricle. In what order does it cross the layers?
  1. A. Fibrous pericardium → visceral serous layer (epicardium) → pericardial cavity → parietal serous layer → myocardium → endocardium
  2. B. Fibrous pericardium → parietal serous layer → pericardial cavity → visceral serous layer (epicardium) → myocardium → endocardium
  3. C. Parietal serous layer → fibrous pericardium → pericardial cavity → visceral serous layer (epicardium) → myocardium → endocardium
AnswerB. Fibrous pericardium → parietal serous layer → pericardial cavity → visceral serous layer (epicardium) → myocardium → endocardium
WhyOutside in: fibrous pericardium → parietal serous layer → cavity → visceral serous layer (= epicardium) → myocardium → endocardium.
Explanation

The pericardium has two parts. The fibrous pericardium is the outer sac. The serous pericardium is a closed double layer of mesothelium: its parietal layer lines the inside of the fibrous sac, then folds back at the great vessels to become the visceral layer, which sticks to the heart surface and is the epicardium. The thin pericardial cavity lies between the two serous layers.

  • Putting the visceral layer first inverts the serous pair: the visceral layer is the one on the heart.
  • Putting the parietal layer outside the fibrous sac is wrong: it lines the fibrous sac from the inside.

Picture a fist (the heart) pushed into a balloon: the layer touching the fist is visceral, the outer balloon wall is parietal.

Under the microscope, what does the epicardium consist of?
  1. A. Mesothelium over loose connective tissue holding fat, nerves and coronary vessels
  2. B. Endothelium over loose connective tissue holding pale Purkinje fibres
  3. C. Dense fibrous tissue over areolar tissue, with no epithelial covering
AnswerA. Mesothelium over loose connective tissue holding fat, nerves and coronary vessels
WhyEpicardium = simple squamous mesothelium on loose connective tissue with fat, autonomic nerves and coronary vessels.
Explanation

On the slide the epicardium is the pale outer band of the heart wall. Its free surface is a single row of flat mesothelial cells. Beneath them is loose connective tissue (fibro-elastic) with fat cells, nerve bundles and branches of the coronary arteries and veins, and below that the cardiac muscle begins.

  • Endothelium with Purkinje fibres describes the endocardium on the inner side of the wall.
  • Dense fibrous tissue without a surface epithelium describes the fibrous pericardium, the outer sac.

L6Learning
What secretes pericardial fluid, and why is it needed?
  1. A. The endothelium of the coronary vessels — it stops the two layers rubbing
  2. B. The mesothelium of the serous pericardium — it stops the two layers rubbing
  3. C. The fibrous pericardium — it nourishes the outer myocardium
AnswerB. The mesothelium of the serous pericardium — it stops the two layers rubbing
WhyThe mesothelial cells of both serous layers secrete a lubricating fluid that lets the beating heart slide inside the sac without friction.
Explanation

Mesothelium is the simple squamous epithelium that lines the body's serous cavities. In the heart it covers the epicardium and lines the parietal pericardium, and it secretes a thin serous fluid into the pericardial cavity. The fluid lubricates the surfaces as the heart moves with each beat.

  • Endothelium lines blood vessels and the heart chambers; it faces blood, not the pericardial cavity.
  • The fibrous pericardium is dense connective tissue with no secretory epithelium, and the fluid does not feed the myocardium (the coronary vessels do).

L7Learning
What is the mechanism of cardiac tamponade?
  1. A. Fluid or blood fills the pericardial cavity → the fibrous sac stretches → the heart dilates and cannot contract
  2. B. Fluid or blood fills the pericardial cavity → the fibrous sac cannot stretch → the heart is squeezed and cannot fill
  3. C. The pericardium scars and thickens → the heart is encased and cannot fill, with no fluid present
AnswerB. Fluid or blood fills the pericardial cavity → the fibrous sac cannot stretch → the heart is squeezed and cannot fill
WhyIn tamponade, fluid in the sac cannot push the inextensible fibrous pericardium outward, so it presses on the heart and stops the chambers filling.
Explanation

The pericardial cavity normally holds only a thin film of fluid. When blood or fluid collects quickly, the fibrous pericardium cannot expand, so the pressure in the sac rises and compresses the heart, first the thin-walled right atrium and ventricle. Less filling means a smaller stroke volume, falling blood pressure and, without drainage, death.

  • If the sac stretched, there would be room for the fluid and no compression; it is the failure to stretch that matters.
  • Scarring and thickening with no fluid is constrictive pericarditis, a different disease that also limits filling.

L8Learning
Two weeks after a massive heart attack, a man has sharp chest pain, BP 80/40 and pulse 100, and dies. Autopsy shows a rupture in the left ventricular wall. What killed him?
  1. A. Pulmonary oedema
  2. B. Cardiac tamponade
  3. C. Pulmonary thromboembolism
AnswerB. Cardiac tamponade
WhyThe softened infarct ruptured; blood poured into the pericardial cavity (haemopericardium) and compressed the heart: cardiac tamponade.
Explanation

After a myocardial infarction the dead muscle is soft while it is being removed and before the scar is strong, typically in the first one to two weeks. A free-wall rupture lets ventricular blood under high pressure flood the pericardial cavity. The inextensible fibrous pericardium cannot expand, so the heart cannot fill: low blood pressure, fast pulse and sudden death.

  • Pulmonary oedema comes from left heart failure raising pulmonary venous pressure; it does not follow from a hole that empties the ventricle into the sac.
  • Pulmonary thromboembolism starts from deep vein thrombi in the legs, not from a ruptured ventricle.

L9Learning
What is constrictive pericarditis?
  1. A. Pericardium filled with fluid and stretched → restricted outflow from the ventricles
  2. B. Pericardium scarred and thickened (over 5 mm), with loss of elasticity → restricted filling of the heart
  3. C. Myocardium scarred and thickened, with loss of elasticity → restricted contraction of the ventricles
AnswerB. Pericardium scarred and thickened (over 5 mm), with loss of elasticity → restricted filling of the heart
WhyIn constrictive pericarditis the pericardium becomes a thick (>5 mm), scarred, inelastic shell that limits venous inflow, with no fluid needed.
Explanation

Chronic inflammation of the pericardium (for example after tuberculosis) replaces it with dense scar tissue, sometimes calcified. The rigid shell stops the heart expanding in diastole, so blood backs up in the veins; the heart looks 'quiet' because it barely moves. The problem is filling, not pumping.

  • Fluid-filled and stretched describes an effusion, and the problem in tamponade is also filling, not outflow.
  • Scarred myocardium is a disease of the muscle; constrictive pericarditis spares the muscle.

How is constrictive pericarditis treated?
  1. A. Pericardiectomy (removing the pericardium)
  2. B. Pericardiocentesis (draining fluid with a needle)
  3. C. Coronary artery bypass grafting
AnswerA. Pericardiectomy (removing the pericardium)
WhyCutting away the rigid shell (pericardiectomy) frees the heart; on imaging its movement returns to normal.
Explanation

The disease is a thick scarred sac, so the cure is to remove it. The lab's moving images show the same patient's heart barely moving before surgery and beating normally after pericardiectomy.

  • Pericardiocentesis (needle drainage) treats tamponade, where the problem is fluid; there is no fluid to drain in constriction.
  • Bypass grafting treats blocked coronary arteries, which are not the problem here.

In a heart section, how can you tell that a layer of flat surface cells is endothelium rather than mesothelium?
  1. A. It faces the pericardial cavity, and the connective tissue beneath has fat and nerve bundles
  2. B. Its cells are cuboidal, while mesothelial cells are flat
  3. C. It faces a chamber lumen, and the connective tissue beneath has no fat or nerve bundles
AnswerC. It faces a chamber lumen, and the connective tissue beneath has no fat or nerve bundles
WhyBoth are flat single layers; the endothelium faces the chamber blood and sits on thin connective tissue without fat or nerves (often with Purkinje fibres below).
Explanation

The cells themselves look the same: one layer of flattened cells with flat nuclei. What gives them away is the company they keep. Endothelium lines the endocardium, faces the lumen of a chamber and sits on thin connective tissue that runs into the subendocardium, where pale Purkinje fibres may appear. Mesothelium covers the epicardium, faces the pericardial cavity and sits on loose tissue with fat lobules, nerves and coronary vessels.

  • Facing the pericardial cavity with fat and nerves below is the description of mesothelium.
  • Cuboidal is wrong: both are simple squamous epithelia.

L12Learning
How are the cardiac muscle cells of the myocardium arranged?
  1. A. In concentric, spiral layers round the chambers, grouped into fascicles by sheets of elastic laminae with no vessels
  2. B. In concentric, spiral layers round the chambers, grouped into fascicles by connective-tissue septa carrying vessels and nerves
  3. C. In straight longitudinal sheets from base to apex, forming one continuous mass with no septa
AnswerB. In concentric, spiral layers round the chambers, grouped into fascicles by connective-tissue septa carrying vessels and nerves
WhyMyocardial cells wind round the chambers in spiral layers; connective-tissue septa carrying vessels and nerves group them into fascicles.
Explanation

The spiral arrangement wrings blood out of the ventricles: when the layers contract, the chamber shortens and twists, squeezing blood toward the outflow valves. Between the fascicles, thin connective-tissue septa carry the rich capillary network and autonomic nerves that the working muscle needs. Ventricular fascicles are larger than atrial ones.

  • Elastic laminae without vessels is wrong twice: the septa are ordinary connective tissue, and they carry the vessels and nerves.
  • Straight longitudinal sheets would only shorten the chamber, not wring it; the slide shows spiral bands.

What do atrial muscle cells store in small secretory granules?
  1. A. Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF)
  2. B. Renin
  3. C. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)
AnswerA. Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF)
WhyAtrial myocytes store the peptide hormone atrial natriuretic factor in small granules near the nucleus, so the atria are also an endocrine organ.
Explanation

When the atria are stretched by a high blood volume, the cells release ANF (also called atrial natriuretic peptide). The granules are tiny and are seen with the electron microscope rather than on routine H&E.

  • Renin is made by the juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney, not by heart muscle.
  • ADH is made by hypothalamic neurons and released from the posterior pituitary.

What does atrial natriuretic factor do?
  1. A. Makes the kidney retain more sodium and water, raising blood volume and pressure
  2. B. Constricts arterioles, raising blood pressure
  3. C. Makes the kidney excrete more sodium and water, lowering blood volume and pressure
AnswerC. Makes the kidney excrete more sodium and water, lowering blood volume and pressure
WhyANF is released when the atria are overstretched and makes the kidneys lose sodium and water (natriuresis), so blood volume and pressure fall.
Explanation

'Natriuretic' means causing sodium loss in the urine. ANF increases the glomerular filtration rate and blocks sodium reabsorption, and it also relaxes vascular smooth muscle. All its effects lower blood volume and pressure, the opposite of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.

  • Retaining sodium and water is what aldosterone and ADH do.
  • Constricting arterioles is the action of angiotensin II and sympathetic stimulation; ANF dilates.

Which three junctions make up an intercalated disc?
  1. A. Fascia adherens
    Desmosomes
    Gap junctions
  2. B. Tight junctions
    Desmosomes
    Gap junctions
  3. C. Fascia adherens
    Hemidesmosomes

AnswerA. Fascia adherens
Desmosomes
Gap junctions
WhyAn intercalated disc is a junctional complex of fasciae adherentes and desmosomes (holding) plus gap junctions (signalling).
Explanation

In the electron microscope the disc runs like a staircase. Its transverse parts (the 'treads') carry the fasciae adherentes, where actin filaments anchor, and the desmosomes, which bind the cells against the pull of contraction.   • Tight junctions seal epithelia; there are none in the intercalated disc.
  • Hemidesmosomes fix a cell to its basement membrane, not to a neighbouring cell.

L17Learning
Intercalated discs contain all of these molecules EXCEPT:
  1. A. Collagen VII
  2. B. Desmoglein and desmocollin
  3. C. Cadherin
AnswerA. Collagen VII
WhyCollagen VII forms anchoring fibrils beneath epithelia; the disc's junctions use desmogleins/desmocollins (desmosomes), cadherins (fascia adherens) and connexins (gap junctions).
Explanation

Each junction of the intercalated disc has its own adhesion proteins. Desmosomes bind neighbouring cells with the cadherin-family proteins desmoglein and desmocollin, held inside by desmoplakin. The fascia adherens uses N-cadherin linked to actin. Gap junctions are built from connexins.

  • The other two options are genuine disc proteins.

Likewise, hemidesmosomes are never part of an intercalated disc.

In cardiac muscle from an elderly person, yellow-brown granules cluster at the poles of the central nuclei. What are they?

Endocardium and Purkinje fibres

L19Learning
What makes up the endocardium?
  1. A. Endothelium → connective tissue with scattered smooth muscle → deeper subendocardial connective tissue
  2. B. Mesothelium → loose connective tissue with fat → deeper subendocardial connective tissue
  3. C. Endothelium resting directly on cardiac muscle, with no connective tissue
AnswerA. Endothelium → connective tissue with scattered smooth muscle → deeper subendocardial connective tissue
WhyEndocardium = endothelium + a middle layer of connective tissue with some smooth muscle + a deeper subendocardium that blends into the myocardium.
Explanation

The inner lining of the heart has three parts: a simple squamous endothelium facing the blood; a middle myoelastic layer of connective tissue with scattered smooth muscle cells; and the subendocardial layer, loose connective tissue continuous with the myocardium's connective tissue. The subendocardium of the ventricles carries the Purkinje fibres.

  • Mesothelium with fat describes the epicardium on the outside of the heart.
  • Endothelium directly on muscle leaves out the connective-tissue layers in which the Purkinje fibres run.

L20Learning
How do Purkinje fibres look compared with ordinary cardiac muscle cells?
  1. A. Smaller and darker — little glycogen, more myofibrils
  2. B. Larger and paler — abundant glycogen, fewer myofibrils
  3. C. Larger and darker — abundant myoglobin, more myofibrils
AnswerB. Larger and paler — abundant glycogen, fewer myofibrils
WhyPurkinje fibres are larger and paler than contractile cells: they are packed with glycogen and have fewer myofibrils.
Explanation

Purkinje fibres are modified cardiac muscle cells built for conduction, not force. Much of their cytoplasm is glycogen instead of myofibrils, so in H&E they look swollen and pale beside the dense pink working muscle; in a trichrome stain they are pale lavender above the deep-red myocardium.

  • Smaller and darker with more myofibrils describes the contractile cells, not the conducting ones.
  • Myoglobin and extra myofibrils would make them darker; their pallor comes from glycogen.

Giveaway: big pale cells just under the endocardium = Purkinje fibres.

How do the intercalated discs of Purkinje fibres differ from typical ones?
  1. A. Fasciae adherentes and desmosomes present; gap junctions absent
  2. B. Tight junctions replace gap junctions; desmosomes well developed
  3. C. Desmosomes and gap junctions present; fasciae adherentes poorly developed

The conduction system

L22Learning
What are the cells of the cardiac conduction system?
  1. A. Modified cardiac muscle cells, specialised for conduction rather than contraction
  2. B. Specialised neurons of the autonomic nervous system
  3. C. Modified smooth muscle cells, specialised for conduction rather than contraction
AnswerA. Modified cardiac muscle cells, specialised for conduction rather than contraction
WhyThe nodes, the AV bundle and the Purkinje fibres are all modified cardiac muscle cells that generate and conduct the impulse instead of contracting strongly.
Explanation

The SA and AV nodes are small masses of specialised myocardial tissue in the right atrial wall; the AV bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje fibres are conducting muscle cells joined by gap junctions. They contain few myofibrils.

  • Neurons: the heart beats without its nerves; autonomic nerves only speed or slow the rate set by the SA node. 'Purkinje cells' of the cerebellum are neurons, but Purkinje fibres of the heart are muscle.
  • Smooth muscle occurs in the endocardium's myoelastic layer and in vessel walls, but it does not conduct the heartbeat.

L23Learning
Trace the cardiac conduction pathway.
  1. A. SA node → AV bundle (of His) → AV node → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres
  2. B. SA node → AV node → AV bundle (of His) → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres
  3. C. AV node → SA node → AV bundle (of His) → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres
AnswerB. SA node → AV node → AV bundle (of His) → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres
WhySA node fires → atria → AV node (delay) → bundle of His through the skeleton → bundle branches in the septum → Purkinje fibres → ventricles.
Explanation

The impulse starts in the SA node in the right atrial wall and spreads over both atria. It is funnelled into the AV node, which delays it, then into the AV bundle (of His), the only path through the insulating cardiac skeleton. The bundle splits into right and left branches in the interventricular septum; about halfway down these become the subendocardial Purkinje network that reaches all of both ventricles.

  • Putting the bundle of His before the AV node skips the delay: the AV node feeds the bundle, not the other way round.
  • Starting at the AV node makes it the pacemaker; the SA node fires first because it is the fastest.

Where is the sinoatrial (SA) node, and what is its role?
  1. A. Wall of the left atrium — pacemaker of the heart
  2. B. Interventricular septum — pacemaker of the heart
  3. C. Wall of the right atrium — pacemaker of the heart
AnswerC. Wall of the right atrium — pacemaker of the heart
WhyThe SA node sits in the right atrial wall near the entrance of the superior vena cava and is the pacemaker: it fires fastest and sets the heart rate.
Explanation

The SA node is a small mass of specialised cardiac muscle under the epicardium at the junction of the superior vena cava with the right atrium. Its cells depolarise spontaneously faster than any other part of the conduction system, so each beat starts there. When it fails, an artificial pacemaker can take over.

L26Learning
Why is the delay at the AV node useful?
  1. A. It lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract
  2. B. It lets the ventricles finish emptying before the atria contract
  3. C. It lets the ventricles contract before the atria fill
AnswerA. It lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract
WhyThe AV-node delay gives the atria time to finish pushing blood into the ventricles before the ventricles contract.
Explanation

The atria contract first and top up the ventricles at the end of their filling. If the impulse reached the ventricles at once, they would contract while the atria were still emptying, and the AV valves would close on incomplete filling.

  • Ventricles emptying before the atria contract reverses the sequence: atrial contraction comes first.
  • Ventricles contracting before the atria fill has nothing to do with the delay; the atria fill throughout ventricular contraction.

The cardiac skeleton

L27Learning
What tissue forms the cardiac skeleton?
  1. A. Dense irregular connective tissue
  2. B. Dense regular connective tissue
  3. C. Elastic connective tissue
AnswerA. Dense irregular connective tissue
WhyThe cardiac skeleton is dense irregular connective tissue: thick collagen bundles running in many directions.
Explanation

The skeleton must resist pull from every direction, as valves snap shut and muscle contracts on all sides, so its collagen bundles are woven in many directions, like the dermis. It lies mainly in the endocardium at the junction of atria and ventricles.

  • Dense regular tissue has parallel bundles that resist pull in one direction only, as in tendons.
  • Elastic tissue stretches and recoils, as in the aortic wall; the skeleton has to stay rigid to hold the valve openings in shape.

How is the cardiac skeleton arranged?
  1. A. Four fibrous rings (annuli) around the four valves, joined by the left and right fibrous trigones

The three tunics and the vasa vasorum

L1Learning
Name the three tunics of a blood-vessel wall, from the lumen outward.
  1. A. Tunica media → tunica intima → tunica adventitia
  2. B. Tunica intima → tunica media → tunica adventitia
  3. C. Tunica adventitia → tunica media → tunica intima
AnswerB. Tunica intima → tunica media → tunica adventitia
WhyFrom the lumen outward: intima (endothelium), media (smooth muscle and elastic tissue), adventitia (connective tissue).
Explanation

A tunic is a concentric coat of the vessel wall. The intima lines the lumen (endothelium plus a thin subendothelial layer); the media is the middle coat of smooth muscle and elastic tissue; the adventitia (tunica externa) is the outer connective-tissue coat that blends with the surrounding tissue.

  • Media first is impossible: the endothelium always faces the blood, and it belongs to the intima.
  • Adventitia first reverses the order; adventitia means the coat that comes from outside.

Almost every vessel-identification question is really asking which of these three coats dominates. Hook: I-M-A, from inside out.
